     The BC Wildfire Service may recommend that 
an evacuation order or evacuation alert be put in 
place, but it generally does not implement, 
manage or rescind it. An evacuation (except a 
“tactical” evacuation) must be authorized by the 
local government. 
      In extreme circumstances, the BC Wildfire 
Service can implement a tactical evacuation 
when there is an imminent risk and there’s no 
time to obtain a formal Evacuation Order. BC 
Wildfire Service personnel have the authority 
under the Wildfire Act to order people to quickly 
leave an area in response to wildfire threats or, if 
necessary, operational requirements. This ability 
is especially valuable when time does not permit 
a local evacuation order or alert to be declared, 
when a wildfire is burning on provincial or 
federal land, or at the request of a First Nation. 
     Pieces of provincial legislation that govern 
evacuations include: Emergency Program Act , 
Wildfire Act, Fire Services Act , Health Act , Mines 
Act, Waste Management Act , Workers 
Compensation Act , and Petroleum and 
Natural Gas Act.

     The area around a wildfire (including everything 
within the fire perimeter and the space above the fire) 
is considered to be a worksite, so the only people 
allowed to be there are those who have been 
authorized by the BC Wildlife Service.  
     If firefighters think it’s necessary, additional areas 
near the wildfire may also be designated as “restricted 
access only”, in order to address public safety 
concerns, limit fire risks or avoid outside interference 
with fire control activities. Any such restrictions are 
applied in accordance with the Wildfire Act  and 
Wildfire Regulation for a specified period of time. 
     When a Restricted Area Order is in effect, people 
must not enter or remain in the designated area. 
Exceptions may be granted if a person obtains written 
authorization from a BC Wildfire Service official, 
usually to enter the area to travel to and from a 
residence or to travel to or from an operation or 
activity that is specifically exempted in the order. 
     Restricted Area Orders may have an impact on 
tourism and industrial operations, so they are only 
applied after serious consideration of the situation on 
the ground. 
     People often don’t realize that the airspace above a 
wildfire is also part of a fire worksite. This allows 
firefighting aircraft to move freely without outside 
interference. If additional airspace is required, the BC 
Wildfire Service may apply for a Notice to Airmen 
(NoTAM) that would enlarge the restricted area.  
     The topic of road closures can be somewhat 
complicated. If a road is going to be closed due 
to wildfire activity and it’s a major highway, it is 
up to the BC Wildfire Service to recommend the 
closure. However, it’s the Ministry of 
Transportation and Infrastructure that oversees 
the closure, monitors it and (in some cases) 
provides flagging or piloting assistance on 
provincial highways.  
      If the road is a forest service road, then it falls 
under the jurisdiction of the Ministry of Forests, 
Lands and Natural Resource Operations, which 
includes the BC Wildfire Service. On the 
recommendation of the BC Wildfire Service, a 
forest district manager (or other designated 
person) and/or the permit holder has the 
authority to close any forest service road.  
      Local road closures (and related security and 
monitoring) are the responsibility of the relevant 
local government. The regional district or 
municipality would be responsible for any road 
closures within its jurisdiction.  
Evacuations 
The area around a forest fire; even with no fire sup-
pression aircraft activity, is considered to be flight 
restricted under the authority of Canadian Aviation 
Regulation 601.15 (over a forest fire area, or over 
any area that is located within 5 nautical miles of a 
forest fire, at an altitude of less than 3000 feet above 
ground level).  
     The BC Wildfire Service urges members of the 
public to respect all of these restrictions and stay 
away from active fire operations. If unauthorized 
people deliberately or mistakenly enter an affected 
area and firefighting staff have to stop their work to 
remove them, they may put both themselves and our 
staff at risk. 

 Heavy Equipment Hiring 
Page 2 
 The BC Wildfire Service encourages heavy 
equipment operators to pre-register with their local 
fire zone so they can be deployed quickly on a fire 
when needed. The most appropriate equipment will 
be selected in each case to ensure that fire 
suppression objectives are met.   
 If additional equipment is required, the BC Wildfire 
Service will hire it in a fair and equitable manner.  
 Every year, the BC Wildfire Service approaches local 
businesses to pre -register for the upcoming fire 
season. Any heavy equipment operator can pre -
register, but he or she must have all the appropriate 
documentation, insurance 
and licences. 
 The BC Wildfire Service has a 
responsibility to be fiscally 
responsible. If more than one 
operator pre -registers (with 
all else being equal), the 
operator who agrees to work 
for Blue Book rates will be 
selected.  
 Operational requirements 
will dictate the size, type and 
attachment requirements of 
the equipment. Equipment 
hired by the BC Wildfire Service must have safety 
and operational attachments in place that are 
appropriate for the conditions encountered. 
 The equipment and operator must be properly 
insured and registered with WorkSafeBC. 
 All equipment must be properly maintained and in 
good working condition to avoid breakdowns and 
damage to the environment. It will also need to 
pass a BC Wildfire Service inspection. 
 Equipment safety requirements include: 
 roll-over protection system 
 falling object protection system 
 safety belts 
 night-capable (lighting) 
 appropriate maintenance 
 escape doors 
 Multiple operators are often used for shift work or 
overnight work, so having two or more operators 
available for each machine is preferable. 
 Having a low -bed trailer available is an advantage, 
since the ability to easily move, mobilize and 
demobilize equipment allows the BC Wildfire 
Service to relocate equipment quickly as needed.  
     The BC Wildfire Service must be able to hire 
and mobilize heavy equipment very quickly if the 
wildfire situation warrants it. Contractors 
interested in doing this sort of work are asked to 
pre-register with their local fire zone early in the 
spring so they can be deployed right away when 
needed, since a delay in mobilizing equipment 
could hamper firefighting efforts.  
     Qualified operators (preferably with 
experience working with fire crews) are also 
needed during the fire season. The BC Wildfire 
Service looks for trained operators who:  
 are used to working in adverse conditions and 
are comfortable doing so 
 are knowledgeable about local terrain and 
soil conditions 
 have appropriate personal protective 
equipment (PPE) 
 have experience operating the equipment 
and are familiar with its maintenance 
requirements 
What you Need to be Hired 
 The availability of supervisory personnel may 
limit the amount of heavy equipment that 
can be used on a given fire site, because 
operators without previous wildfire 
experience may not be aware of: a fire’s rate 
of spread; how weather can impact fire 
behaviour; volumes and types of unburned 
fuels within the fireline; the fire cycle; and 
the risk of sudden increases in fire intensity.  
 Equipment operators must work in 
conjunction with line locators, who use their 
knowledge of fire behaviour to keep fire 
crews and equipment operators safe.  
 Operator experience, reliability of equipment 
and supervisory limits are all factors that 
contribute to a successful firefighting 
operation.   
 The primary function of heavy equipment in 
wildfire suppression is using the appropriate 
specialized equipment. The best tool for the 
job will always be selected.

 Heavy Equipment 
Page 3 
 
Dozer: A dozer (also referred to as a bulldozer or cat) 
is the most common piece of heavy equipment used 
on a wildfire. Dozers do the bulk of line construction 
on large project fires, especially if the terrain is 
difficult. They can move a lot of earth in a short 
amount of time. The end product is much more 
effective in containing a fire than a line that crews dig 
by hand. Dozers can help build access roads, helipads 
and safety zones, and they can also push over danger 
trees and flatten areas to store portable water sources. 
 
Excavator: Excavators are often used to clean an area 
of excess debris and help create fire breaks. 
Excavators can also be used to dig out stumps and 
roots and lift debris as necessary. This is a very 
versatile tool with a large mouth shovel on the end 
boom arm that performs whatever job is required. The 
shovel can be replaced by a variety of attachments 
which makes the excavator a very versatile tool. 
 
Skidder: A skidder has two key uses on a fire line. 
Skidders can come equipped with water tanks and 
deliver water to precise locations, as operator skill and 
terrain allow. They can also drop the tank and work as 
they were built to do in the logging industry, by 
collecting and removing (i.e. skidding) wood. This is 
done in conjunction with a feller buncher. Line 
skidders or grapple skidders may be used.  
 
Feller buncher : Feller bunchers are becoming more 
common on the fire line. Their sole purpose is to fall 
and bunch wood for skidding, in advance of a dozer 
pushing in the fire guard. This machine allows timber 
to be salvaged as the control lines are constructed. 
Their use also reduces the amount of mess created 
(and the difficulty of rehabilitating the area once the 
fire is out).  


     The BC Wildfire Service is always on the lookout for new and 
better ways to do the job so the opportunity to review new 
technology is cause for excitement in the Coastal Fire Centre.  
     The MD Helicopters  MD Explorer is a light twin utility 
helicopter. Designed in the early 1990s by McDonnell Douglas 
Helicopter Systems , it is currently produced by MD Helicopters, 
Inc. There have been two models, the original MD 900, and its 
successor, the MD 902. 
     Crews were given the chance to hover exit from the 
MD900. These helicopters have Notar technology which 
means that they have ‘No Tail Rotor’. The helicopter has a 
fan in the tail which builds a high volume of low pressure 
air, which blows out the two vents, hugging the tailboom 
and providing lift. The technology utilizes the Coandă 
effect.  
     Although this helicopter has not yet been approved for 
use by BCWS crews it is a great 
opportunity for crews to check 
out the aircraft.  
 
      It has been very quiet for Coastal fire fighters 
with only 4 small fires in the Fire Centre. One 
Unit Crew was kept busy over the last few days 
working on a fire in the Pemberton zone. This fire 
is 8 kilometres southwest of Pemberton and is 
2.8 hectares in size. The fire was reported on July 
27, 2016. Airtankers and a helicopter provided 
support on the first day. Today 13 fire fighters 
remain on this fire with one piece of heavy 
equipment. The crew is making good progress 
and expect that the fire will move into Patrol 
status soon and continue to be patrolled over the 
long weekend.    
     Crews throughout the Fire Centre are 
continuing work on a variety of projects for 
communities and local governments.

SYNOPSIS: (Today-tomorrow) Moderate upper ridge 
pattern bringing the current warm dry spell hangs on 
for another day and then collapses over the weekend. 
There is marginal instability expected over the Coast 
Mountains late this afternoon which brings a chance of 
isolated convection along Coastal’s eastern border. 
Tonight the upper trough coming down from BC’s north 
coast brings a few clouds and an increase in winds. No 
showers are likely as the trough crosses all zones 
tomorrow by afternoon. There will, however, be an 
increase in instability all along the Coast Mountains so 
perhaps we see an isolated shower late tomorrow and a 
chance of a thunderstorm. More certain is a general fall 
off in temperatures and stronger afternoon winds. 
Winds likely reach 20 to 25 km/h all along the coast and 
through the main river valleys.  
OUTLOOK: (Sunday-Tuesday) Sunday is a day in 
between the first weak trough and ahead of the next 
approaching low so stays sunny and dry. Monday, the 
next low centre drifts down the coast and brings clouds 
and a few showers. The low passes over early Tuesday 
so the showers intensify late Monday and ease by 
Tuesday afternoon. Current forecasts show the south -
eastern Fraser zone receiving up to 20 mm 
of rain.
