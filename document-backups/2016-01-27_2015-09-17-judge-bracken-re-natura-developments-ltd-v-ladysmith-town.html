Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 2 
[1] In December 2014, the petitioner Natura Developments Ltd. (“Natura”) 
submitted a development permit application respecting property it owned in the 
Town of Ladysmith (“Ladysmith”).  Natura wanted permission to develop 27 
townhouse units.  The existing zoning applicable to the property allowed for 29 units.  
Natura asserted that its plan was consistent with the existing zoning, the Official 
Community Plan (“OCP”) and the Development Permit Guidelines of Ladysmith. 
[2] Ladysmith planning officials advised Natura that the proposed development 
was inconsistent with a restrictive covenant that was registered against the title to 
the land that limited development to 15 townhouse units.  Natura was aware of the 
restrictive covenant but said that it intended to apply to court for an order cancelling 
that covenant.  Ladysmith then passed bylaw 1875 to change the zoning of the 
property to limit development to 15 units. 
[3] In this action, Natura seeks orders quashing Ladysmith bylaw number 1875, 
on the grounds of illegality.  By its further amended petition, the petitioner also seeks 
an order quashing Ladysmith bylaw 1881, which is a subsequent bylaw that 
repealed bylaw 1875 and replaced it with a bylaw of essentially identical terms. 
[4] Natura also seeks an order that restrictive covenant No. EX606846 (“the 
covenant”) registered against the title of property be cancelled.  Natura relies on 
s. 35(2) of the Property Law Act, R.S.B.C. 1996, c. 377 and says that the covenant 
is obsolete and is impeding the reasonable use of the property without practical 
benefit to others.  The petitioner also says that the covenant is invalid, 
unenforceable or expired. 
[5] Ladysmith was apparently concerned the covenant might not withstand a 
legal challenge, so it prepared and passed bylaw 1875 which had the effect of 
rezoning the property to be consistent with the terms of the development density 
allowed by the covenant.  The bylaw targeted only Natura’s property which, but for 
the covenant, is within a zone that allowed greater development density consistent 
with Natura’s development application.  If the covenant was cancelled, the new “spot 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 3 
zoning” bylaw 1875 was intended to replace the covenant and preserve the 
development density that had been established. 
[6] Natura then sought to quash the bylaw.  It advanced several grounds.  It 
alleged the bylaw was an inappropriate use of the town’s zoning power in that it was 
a “spot zoning” bylaw that singled out only Natura’s property.  Natura also alleged 
that the bylaw was in conflict with Ladysmith’s OCP.  Finally, Natura alleged that it 
had not been given proper notice of the bylaw as required by The Local Government 
Act, R.S.B.C. 1996, c. 323, s. 892. 
[7] Ladysmith acknowledged that formal notice of the public hearing under the 
Local Government Act was not properly delivered to Natura.  But Ladysmith did not 
admit that Natura was unaware of the public hearing related to the bylaw and 
seemed to reserve the right to argue that a reasonable effort was made to provide 
notice to Natura such that the bylaw might survive a challenge on the ground of 
failure to give proper notice to Natura. 
[8] Nevertheless, Ladysmith then passed a new bylaw, No. 1881, that is identical 
in its terms to bylaw 1875 as to the zoning restrictions except that it repealed bylaw 
1875.  There is no procedural challenge to bylaw 1881.  Natura has amended its 
petition to place both bylaws 1875 and 1881 under challenge. 
[9] Natura challenges bylaw 1881 on the ground that Ladysmith misused its 
zoning power to rezone the property and on the ground that the bylaw is contrary to 
the OCP.  Effectively, both bylaws remain under challenge.  If bylaw 1881 is 
repealed, bylaw 1875 would be briefly revived, but would no doubt succumb to the 
same challenge that quashed bylaw 1881. 
[10] Because the first problem is the covenant and in the interests of limiting the 
confusion associated with the bylaw challenges, I directed that the application to 
cancel the covenant be determined before further argument is heard respecting the 
challenge to the bylaws.

Discussion 
[26] As already noted, Natura applies for an order cancelling the covenant under 
s. 35 of the Property Law Act.  Section 35(1)(e) grants the court authority to modify 
or cancel a restrictive covenant, and s. 35(2) sets out five grounds on which such 
modification or cancellation may be ordered.  The relevant provisions say: 
35  (1) A person interested in land may apply to the Supreme Court for an 
order to modify or cancel any of the following charges or interests against the 
land, whether registered before or after this section comes into force: 
 … 
 (e) a restrictive or other covenant burdening the land or the 
owner; 
 … 
(2) The court may make an order under subsection (1) on being satisfied 
that the application is not premature in the circumstances, and that 
 (a) because of changes in the character of the land, the 
neighbourhood or other circumstances the court considers material, 
the registered charge or interest is obsolete, 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 10 
 (b) the reasonable use of the land will be impeded, without 
practical benefit to others, if the registered charge or interest is not 
modified or cancelled, 
 (c) the persons who are or have been entitled to the benefit of the 
registered charge or interest have expressly or impliedly agreed to it 
being modified or cancelled, 
 (d) modification or cancellation will not injure the person entitled to 
the benefit of the registered charge or interest, or 
 (e) the registered instrument is invalid, unenforceable or has 
expired, and its registration should be cancelled. 
Court may modify or cancel charges 
[27] Section 35(2)(c) has no application to these facts, as Natura does not 
contend that Ladysmith agreed to modification or cancellation of the covenant.  I will 
address each of the remaining s. 35(2) grounds below. 
[28] Before doing so, however, I will briefly address the law respecting the 
interpretation of the covenant.  The issue was considered recently in McCorquodale 
v. Baranti Developments Ltd., 2015 BCCA 133, where it was held that a court must 
determine the meaning of a grant by reference only to its plain language, except for 
where that wording creates an ambiguity, or the circumstances demonstrate that the 
parties could not have intended a particular use (para. 23).  In that case the court 
was interpreting an easement. 
[29] In interpreting a grant, the court may refer to the grant’s recitals so as to 
permit identification of “the nature and scope of the grant and any facts on which it is 
based.”  While in certain circumstances the recitals themselves may give rise to 
formal obligations, in general they should be used only to clarify ambiguity 
(paras. 25-26). 
Section 35(2)(a):  Is the Covenant Obsolete? 
[30] Natura urges this court to cancel the covenant for obsolescence under 
s. 35(2)(a) of the Property Law Act.  It does not seek modification of the covenant. 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 11 
[31] In argument, Natura does not contend that the character of the land or 
neighbourhood have changed in a way which renders the covenant obsolete.  
Rather, the petitioner says that other material circumstances make the covenant 
obsolete thereby warranting cancellation. 
[32] Primarily, Natura submits that the covenant is obsolete because it serves a 
defunct purpose, namely to “commit a previous developer to a particular multi-unit 
phased development.”  As noted, that development never came to fruition.  Natura 
further says that the covenant is obsolete because it charges only a portion of the 
property, and cannot restrict the petitioner from using “all of the available density” in 
the areas to which it does not apply. 
[33] The parties agree that in applying s. 35(2)(a), the court must give the term 
“obsolete” its ordinary meaning.  In Portrait Homes Ltd. v. Strata Plan LMS 1191, 
2002 BCCA 257, the Court adopted the following definition (para. 23): 
1.  That is no longer practised or used; discarded; out of date.  2.  Worn out; 
effaced through wearing down, atrophy, or degeneration. 
[34] The court acknowledged in Portrait Homes that the intended use of property 
should be considered in determining whether a charge is obsolete under s. 35(2)(a) 
(paras. 25, 36).  The court also held that the test for obsolescence does not involve 
“balancing the interests” of the parties (paras. 24, 31); as such balancing would not 
assist in determining whether the covenant is out of date, no longer used, or worn 
out. 
[35] Ladysmith places significant reliance on Portrait Homes, where the court 
ultimately reversed a finding of obsolescence made by a chambers judge respecting 
certain construction and access easements. The court stated at para. 36: 
Since I have concluded that the chambers judge erred in her interpretation of 
the Agreement and, in particular, in her conclusion that the Agreement did not 
permit development of the Phase II lands other than in the way originally 
planned, I am also unable to accept her conclusion that the access and 
construction easements under the Agreement are obsolete.  She found that 
these easements were obsolete because the use Portrait proposed to make 
of the easements under the proposed development was substantially different 
from the original use contemplated by the Agreement and, therefore, the 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 12 
easements could no longer serve the original purpose for which they were 
intended.  In my view, the uses Portrait intends to make of the easements are 
essentially the same as the uses intended by the parties to the Agreement, 
namely, reasonable access over the Common Property, including access 
through the underground garage to Buena Vista and for construction 
purposes (subject to any reasonable rules or regulations governing access to 
the Common Property that the Owners may require).  It follows that the 
easements cannot be said to be obsolete within the meaning of the decided 
authorities. 
[36] In my view, Portrait Homes offers only general guidance, as the construction 
and access easements at issue in that case differ significantly from the covenant at 
issue here.  Nevertheless, that case appears to stand for the proposition that a court 
should find a charge to be obsolete only where it can no longer serve its original, 
intended purpose. 
[37] Somewhat more helpful is the decision in Murrayfield Developments Ltd. v. 
Brandon (1995), 8 B.C.L.R. (3d) 364 (S.C.).  In that case the petitioner sought 
cancellation of a restrictive covenant under s. 31 (now s. 35) of the Property Law 
Act.  The covenant in question, which applied to certain land owned by the 
petitioner, barred the petitioner’s development plans by prohibiting subdivision of the 
land and construction of additional buildings on it. 
[38] The preamble to the covenant in Murrayfield stated that it was agreed to “with 
a view to creating a general building scheme and to maintaining the character of ... 
the subdivision.”  Under that covenant, the owner of the land agreed not to 
(para. 12): 
1. Erect or build on any of the said lands any building other than one 
private dwelling house together with a garage and/or other suitable 
outbuildings appurtenant to the said dwelling per lot as the same is now 
defined. 
... 
6. Subdivide any of the said lots nor alienate any part thereof or do any 
act which might result directly or indirectly in a division of the said lot or 
defeat the restrictions, reservations, conditions and covenants hereof. 
[39] By the time the petition was filed, the covenant had applied to the land for 
40 or 50 years.  The petitioner argued that the covenant was obsolete because the 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 13 
surrounding neighbourhood had changed considerably in the intervening decades.  
The Court declined to give effect to that argument, finding that the precise purpose 
of the covenant was to “control density,” a factor which had become more rather 
than less relevant as a result of the changes to the neighbourhood.  As a result, it 
could not be said that the original purpose of the covenant “could no longer be 
served” (paras. 35-39). 
[40] The covenant in this case appears to serve a somewhat different purpose 
than the one at issue in Murrayfield.  On a plain reading of the whole of the 
covenant, it does not merely “control density.”  Rather, it binds the owner of the 
property to a specific process for seeking to develop the property, i.e., the process 
set out in s. 3 of the covenant and enforced through the restrictions in s. 1.  The 
covenant also controls density, however, by preventing development of the property 
beyond or following development under the s. 3 process (s. 4). 
[41] I do not agree with Natura’s suggestion that the covenant binds the owner of 
the property to a specific, now-defunct development plan.  The language employed 
does not support that interpretation, particularly as there appears to be nothing in the 
covenant to prevent Natura from seeking approval of a new development plan under 
the process set out in s. 3 of the covenant.  On the other hand, even if the covenant 
does bind Natura to Ms. Kelt’s particular multi-unit phased development plan, there 
is no evidence before me to suggest that this plan constitutes an obsolete or 
otherwise inappropriate use of the property, or that Natura could not pursue 
development under the plan. 
[42] If I am wrong on those points, the covenant nevertheless serves another 
purpose in controlling the density of the neighborhood through the prohibition in s. 4.  
The petitioner concedes as much in arguing that the “purpose of the covenant in this 
case is an intended restriction or control on development.”  As was the case in 
Murrayfield, there is nothing in the evidence before me to suggest that 
circumstances have subsequently rendered that purpose obsolete. 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 14 
[43] Specifically, the fact that configuration of the property has changed since the 
imposition of the covenant does not itself render the covenant obsolete.  After all, the 
covenant contemplates that very thing in s. 10, providing that its restrictions run with 
the land notwithstanding subdivision into smaller parcels or consolidation into larger. 
[44] I am not persuaded by Natura’s submission that the covenant is obsolete 
merely because it “cannot and does not restrict the petitioner using all of the 
available density on the area of the property not charged by it.”  The mere fact that 
Natura can build on part of the property not charged by the covenant does not 
subvert or render obsolete the purposes of the covenant, which, as discussed 
above, are to bind the owner of the property to a specific process for development 
approval and to control density. 
[45] If Natura built every portion of the property not charged by the covenant to 
maximum density, that would likely enhance rather than extinguish the vitality of 
Ladysmith’s interest in controlling density or the development approval process in 
the remaining property.  In the absence of any convincing evidence to the contrary, I 
cannot give effect to Natura’s submission on this point. 
[46] I also take guidance from the decision of this court in Parker v. Kamloops 
(City), 2012 BCSC 61.  The petitioners in Parker were spouses who owned two 
adjacent lots located within the bounds of the respondent municipality.  In 1992, they 
applied to reconfigure their lot boundaries, reducing the size of one and increasing 
the size of the other.  The proposed reconfiguration violated a minimum lot size 
provision under the applicable zoning bylaw.  However, Kamloops approved the 
application, subject to a restrictive covenant being placed against both lots restricting 
further subdivision. 
[47] The petitioners then applied in 2011 for cancellation of the covenant on a 
number of grounds, including obsolescence under s. 35(2)(a) of the Property Law 
Act.  The Court found, however, that the concerns served by the covenant, related to 
the sufficiency of Kamloops’ water supply, remained relatively unchanged in the 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 15 
interim.  As the result, the court concluded that the covenant still served “its original 
and intended purpose” (paras. 57-59). 
[48] Similar reasoning applies in this case.  The covenant continues to serve its 
intended purpose, namely, granting Ladysmith control over the nature and density of 
development occurring on the property.  Neither the evidence nor the submissions 
persuade me that these interests have since become superfluous. 
[49] I do not find that the covenant is obsolete and decline to cancel it under 
s. 35(2)(a) of the Property Law Act.  I leave open the question of whether the spot-
zoning bylaws, if enforceable, make the covenant obsolete, as that issue was not 
raised by the parties.  
Sections 35(2)(b) and (d):  Does the Covenant Impede the Reasonable Use of 
the Property Without Practical Benefit to Others?  Would Altering the 
Covenant Injure the Persons Entitled to its Benefit? 
[50] Natura seeks cancellation of the covenant under ss. 35(2)(b) and (d) of the 
Property Law Act.  It submits that the covenant impedes the reasonable use of the 
property without practical benefit to others, and says that cancellation of the 
covenant will not injure Ladysmith. 
[51] In support, Natura argues that the covenant does not apply to all of the 
property, nor does it reduce the available density on the unencumbered portions of 
the property.  According to Natura, the zoning of the property at the time the 
development permit application was made permitted construction of 29 dwelling 
units.  Given the restrictions in the covenant, all 29 units would have to be built in 
areas not encumbered by the covenant, with the result being that those areas would 
be used more intensively than they would be if there was no covenant.  That is 
because, absent the covenant, Natura could construct the 29 units anywhere on the 
property instead of only on the unencumbered portions. 
[52] Natura submits that cancellation of the covenant does not injure Ladysmith 
because Ladysmith remains capable of considering “the needs of the community 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 16 
and the public good” through zoning and the development permit process.  On that 
argument the covenant “adds nothing” to these processes and no one is injured by 
its removal. 
[53] Natura further takes the position that third parties such as neighbours lack 
“standing to enforce a covenant between a municipality and an owner,” relying on 
Nancy Green's Olympic Lodge Ltd. Partnership v. Blackcomb Development Ltd., 
[1986] B.C.J. No. 1479 (S.C.) as authority for that proposition.  Natura says that 
cancellation of the covenant does not injure third parties, because they have no right 
to enforce it. 
[54] The Court of Appeal discussed the s. 35(2)(b) analysis in Wallster v. 
Erschbamer, 2011 BCCA 27.  The court in that case was of the view that provision 
does not involve a balancing of interests, but rather requires, first, that there be an 
interference with a reasonable use of the land by a charge or covenant, and second, 
that the “continuation of the covenant or other charge provides no practical benefit” 
[emphasis in original].  In this context, “practical benefit” encompasses both 
objective and subjective factors, including “neighbourhood aesthetics” (para. 19), or 
density:  Gubbels v. Anderson, [1995] 8 W.W.R. 263 (B.C.C.A.), at paras. 23-25. 
[55] Assuming that the construction of 29 dwelling units on the property 
constitutes a reasonable use, I accept that the covenant impedes such use by 
restricting where or perhaps even whether, Natura can construct those units.  The 
question, then, is whether the impediment to development provides any “practical 
benefit to others.” 
[56] In my view, the covenant provides practical benefits to Ladysmith, by binding 
the owner of the property to the specific process for development approval set out in 
s. 3, and also by granting Ladysmith the ability to limit density and development in 
the areas encumbered by the covenant.  The covenant benefits Ladysmith because 
it allows Ladysmith to better ensure that the property is used consistently with 
community needs and the public good. 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 17 
[57] Despite Natura’s submissions, I am not persuaded that these benefits are 
redundant in light of Ladysmith’s ability to otherwise control development or density 
of the property through zoning and the development permit process.  The processes 
provided under the covenant differ from those otherwise available to Ladysmith both 
in function and form.  Furthermore, Ladysmith’s zoning and permit powers relate to 
its ability to regulate land use generally, rather than specifically in relation to the 
property, which of course is the exact effect of the covenant. 
[58] I again refer to Parker, where the petitioners also sought cancellation of a 
covenant under s. 35(2)(b) of the Property Law Act.  The covenant in that case 
prohibited further subdivision of certain land owned by the petitioners, which the 
respondent municipality, Kamloops, construed as granting a “practical benefit” 
because it allowed for control and protection of the municipality’s water supply. 
[59] The petitioners in Parker contended that the covenant was superfluous and 
therefore of no practical benefit, because Kamloops otherwise had “authority to deny 
sub-division approval in circumstances where water capacity is a problem” 
(para. 61).  The court rejected that argument, noting that the covenant in question 
had been entered into “as a pre-condition of approving the zoning by-law 
amendment the petitioners sought in 1992.”  The concerns which led to that 
precondition had not been diminished or extinguished in the intervening years 
(para. 63). 
[60] That circumstance is similar to what happened in this case, where Ladysmith 
made rezoning of the property from R2 to R3A contingent upon Ms. Kelt 
encumbering the property with the covenant.  I again pause to note that the 
evidence does not indicate that this amounted to a quid pro quo exchange between 
Ladysmith and Ms. Kelt. 
[61] Given the purposes served by the covenant, it is clear that Ladysmith 
imposed the covenant as a precondition to rezoning in order to better control density 
and development of the property.  The evidence here discloses “no reduction in the 
basis” for those concerns, to borrow the language deployed in Parker. 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 18 
[62] In my view, the neighbours, Cleve Carleton, Angela and Adrian Salahub, and 
Murray Hanna also derive a practical benefit from the covenant, notwithstanding 
whether they can legally enforce the charge.  Section 35(2)(b) of the Property Law 
Act provides only the qualifier “without practical benefit to others.” It does not say 
“without practical benefit to others entitled to enforce the covenant.”  The 
neighbours’ lack of standing is germane only under s. 35(2)(d). 
[63] As the evidence establishes, the practical benefit to the neighbours comes in 
the form of decreased density, increased privacy and controlled development of the 
property, which are factors properly considered when a party applies to cancel a 
covenant under s. 35(2)(b): Gubbels, at para. 25. 
[64] This benefit also flows to Ladysmith, because by permitting control of 
development and density of the property for the benefit of these persons, Ladysmith 
fulfills its objectives in protecting or responding to community needs.  The Supreme 
Court of Canada acknowledged the legitimacy of those objectives in Saint-Romuald 
(City) v. Olivier, 2001 SCC 57, describing them as a “municipality's interest in having 
all of the land within its boundaries organized in a plan which [the municipality] thinks 
will maximize the benefits and amenities for all inhabitants” (para. 13). 
[65] Similarly, in Parker, the court recognized that the respondent municipality 
obtained a practical benefit in the form of its ability to secure and maintain the 
municipal water supply for the use of its inhabitants (paras. 63-64).  Thus, 
cancellation of the covenant would injure Ladysmith as it would deprive Ladysmith of 
the practical benefits described above:  see Murrayfield, at paras. 44-45. 
[66] Accordingly, I decline to cancel the covenant under s. 35(2)(b) because I find 
that it provides practical benefits to both Ladysmith and the neighbours of the 
property.  I also decline to cancel the covenant under s. 35(2)(d), because to remove 
the covenant would injure Ladysmith.

Section 35(2)(e): Is the Covenant Invalid, Unenforceable, or Expired? 
[67] Natura also asserts that the covenant is invalid, unenforceable, or expired, 
submitting that it should be cancelled for vagueness, uncertainty, or illegality, relying 
on s. 35(2)(e) of the Property Law Act. 
[68] One of the requirements of a restrictive covenant is that its terms must be 
clear:  585582 B.C. Ltd. v. Anderson, 2015 BCCA 261 (para. 21).  Groberman J. (as 
he then was) discussed the analysis under s. 35(2)(e) in Winmark Capital Inc. v. 
Galiano Island Local Trust Committee, 2004 BCSC 1754.  He held that merely 
because the terms of a covenant may be subject to “some debate” does not serve to 
make the covenant unenforceable.  The courts may assist parties in interpreting a 
covenant should that become necessary (para. 24). 
[69] Natura submits that the prohibitions in s. 1 of the covenant preclude “all 
subdivision, construction of buildings, application for new building permits ... in 
perpetuity,” arguing that this “effectively sterilizes” the property.  It says that the 
covenant accordingly lacks the certainty required to be enforceable. 
[70] I respectfully disagree with that characterization.  As discussed above in 
reference to s. 35(2)(a), the covenant merely binds the owner of the property to a 
specific process for obtaining development approval.  Nothing in the covenant 
suggests that this process is now unavailable to Natura, or, even if it were 
unavailable, that Natura could not instead pursue development under Ms. Kelt’s 
multi-unit phased development plan.  The covenant does not, in other words, render 
the property sterile. 
[71] On the other hand, even if the covenant does render the property sterile, I do 
not accept that this necessarily makes the covenant unenforceable.  Indeed, in 
Lorne Park (Re) (1913), 18 D.L.R. 595 (Ont. Sup. Ct.), aff’d (1914), 33 O.L.R. 51 
(Sup. Ct. (A.D.)), the Court considered the argument that a provision in the deeds of 
conveyance of certain lands that had been sub-divided “intended to sterilise for all 
time” 25 acres of adjacent property that it had set aside for use as “picnic grounds” 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 20 
for the benefit of the purchasers (paras. 10, 23).  The Court concluded that this 
amounted to an enforceable implied covenant granting rights that “may exist in 
perpetuity” (para. 24). 
[72] In any event, sterility does not equate to uncertainty.  The covenant applies a 
readily discernible scheme:  it employs a broad prohibition of development (ss. 1 and 
4), subject to a narrow exception exercisable at Ladysmith’s discretion (ss. 2 and 3).  
However, Natura further contends that the covenant lacks certainty precisely 
because the exception in s. 2 is left to the sole discretion of Ladysmith.  In this 
regard the petitioner relies on 585582 and Sekretov v. Toronto (City), [1973] 2 O.R. 
161 (C.A.). 
[73] In Sekretov, the court considered the enforceability of a restrictive covenant 
that, among other things, restricted use of the land “for any ... purpose” other than 
that “provided by resolution of Council” (para. 5). The judge of first instance ruled 
that the covenant was invalid because it did not specify the dominant land which 
benefited from the covenant.  The court affirmed that ruling on appeal (paras. 13, 
18).  However, it then went on to say in obiter that the covenant was also too 
uncertain to be enforceable because it made the use of the land dependent “upon 
the whim of Council to be expressed in a resolution or resolutions to be passed at 
some future time.” (para. 19). 
[74] The Court of Appeal cited Sekretov approvingly in 585582.  In the latter 
decision, the court noted that, like Sekretov, the covenant under consideration 
essentially invoked or incorporated the “whim” of a third party.  This made the 
covenant uncertain, because a third party could unilaterally change its terms at any 
time (para. 30). 
[75] 585582 involved a restrictive covenant registered against strata lots in a 
resort on Lake Okanagan.  The covenant prohibited rental of the strata lots to the 
public except in accordance with a “rental pool management agreement.” In finding 
the covenant void for uncertainty, the court noted that the covenant itself did not 
include the terms of the rental agreement, so that a successor in title could not look 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 21 
to the registry to learn under what terms the lots could be rented to the public.  
Further, those terms had to be negotiated by each owner, and the covenant lacked 
any means for establishing them independently, rendering “a central aspect of the 
covenant” an unenforceable agreement to agree (paras. 26-28). 
[76] I find both Sekretov and 585582 to be distinct from the case at bar. In the 
former, the covenant at issue prohibited “any” alterations to the surface or “any” use 
of the land absent an authorizing resolution from the municipality.  A successor in 
title who encountered that covenant would be unable to discern from its text what 
use (if any) the land could serve.  At any time, the permitted use of the land could 
change if the municipality passed a resolution to that effect. 
[77] That is very different from the covenant, which does not expressly prohibit 
any use of the property except in binding approval of future development, including 
subdivision and construction, to the process set out in s. 3, and controlling density of 
the property thereafter (s. 4).  Nor does the covenant permit Ladysmith to arbitrarily 
or unilaterally change the permitted use of the property. Ladysmith can approve a 
new use of the property, of course, under s. 3 of the covenant, but only upon 
application by the owner.  It can hardly be said that this scheme is uncertain.  It 
merely establishes a procedure under which an owner can request and Ladysmith 
can approve or reject a proposed use of the property. 
[78] In 585582, the covenant in question permitted rental of strata lots only in 
accordance with the terms of an external agreement, which was neither attached to 
nor incorporated into the covenant.  The rental manager could unilaterally change 
the terms of that agreement, and the covenant lacked any mechanism by which the 
terms of the agreement could be established (paras. 26-30).  The covenant’s 
reliance on the external agreement therefore effectively granted the rental manager 
the ability to alter the terms of the covenant at will. 
[79] Again, that is very different from the covenant here, which does not rely on 
the terms of any external, unilaterally alterable document.  Rather, the mechanism 
for establishing whether development may occur is Ladysmith’s sole discretion, a 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 22 
term clearly and unambiguously established in the covenant.  The covenant does 
not provide Ladysmith with unilateral authority to do anything; even the rejection of a 
development plan under s. 3 must be construed as a bilateral act, as of course there 
would be nothing to reject if the owner never requested development to begin with. 
[80] Unlike either 585582 or Sekretov, a successor in title need not look beyond 
the covenant to understand the rights and obligations imposed by that encumbrance.  
In my view, the scheme respecting development approval and density control set out 
under the covenant is clear and unambiguous.  I decline to cancel the covenant for 
uncertainty. 
[81] Natura further contends that the covenant is illegal and subject to cancellation 
under s. 35(2)(e) because it grants Ladysmith rights under contract which are 
inconsistent with its statutory grant of authority.  In particular, Natura points out that 
the covenant purports to grant Ladysmith “private rights” unfettered by any “public 
law duty of fairness or natural justice.” 
[82] There appear to be two aspects to this argument -- first, that the covenant 
fetters Ladysmith’s statutory authority, and second that Ladysmith acquired rights 
through the covenant that it was not statutorily entitled to have. 
[83] Natura refers to a number of authorities in support, beginning with Vancouver 
v. Registrar Vancouver Land Registration District, [1955] 2 D.L.R. 709 (B.C.C.A.).  
That case stands for the proposition that public officials with legal duties cannot 
bargain away their obligations to fulfil their functions (para. 26).  However, it involved 
a circumstance in which a municipality promised without qualification to rezone 
certain lands in exchange for the owner’s execution of a restrictive covenant.  This 
quid pro quo arrangement unlawfully fettered the municipal council’s ability to 
consider the merits of the zoning bylaw (para. 22).  That is a very different 
circumstance from the present case. 
[84] The petitioner also directed me to Pacific National Investments Ltd. v. Victoria 
(City), 2000 SCC 64, another case involving a municipality committing to exercise its 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 23 
zoning powers in a manner that limited its legislative discretion (paras. 65-66).  
LeBel J., for the majority, emphasized the importance of ensuring that municipalities 
are able to exercise zoning powers so as to “reflect [the municipality’s] best wisdom” 
and its “objective examination of what is best for the community” (para. 64). 
[85] Ultimately, Pacific National Investments stands for the proposition that the 
municipality in that case lacked capacity to contract to maintain certain zoning or, if it 
did not, it had to compensate the owner of the lands that were subject to the zoning 
(paras 30, 74).  Again, that is a very different circumstance from the present case. 
[86] There is nothing to suggest in this case that the covenant fetters Ladysmith’s 
statutory or legislative authority in the manner prescribed in the authorities.  That is 
particularly so in light of s. 9, which expressly stipulates that the terms of the 
covenant do not affect or limit Ladysmith’s discretion, rights, duties, or powers, and 
s. 8, which provides that the covenant does not impose any duty of any kind on 
Ladysmith, and provides only permissive rights. 
[87] Ladysmith may freely exercise the discretion conferred in s. 3 of the covenant 
in a manner which reflects its “best wisdom.”  Put differently, Ladysmith remains at 
liberty to conduct an objective examination of what is best for the community in 
considering whether to accept the owner’s application for development approval. 
[88] Further, if the covenant itself no longer reflects what is best for the 
community, Ladysmith may freely consent to its removal under s. 35(2)(c) of the 
Property Law Act and s. 219(9)(b) of the Land Title Act, R.S.B.C. 1996, c. 250. 
[89] Section 8 of the covenant, which purports to exclude application of natural 
justice and the public law duty of fairness from Ladysmith’s exercise of discretion 
under the covenant, does not interfere or fetter Ladysmith’s ability to make decisions 
in a manner consistent with LeBel J.’s ruling in Pacific National Investments.  That 
portion of s. 8, presuming for the moment that it is enforceable, merely prevents the 
owner of the property from insisting on natural justice or the duty of fairness.  

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 24 
Ladysmith remains at liberty to render a decision consistent with those doctrines to 
the extent that this is consistent with its statutory grant of authority. 
[90] Further, the Land Title Act expressly authorizes the registration of covenants 
which favour a municipality in s. 219(1), including covenants which stipulate that 
land “is not to be built on except in accordance with the covenant” (s. 219(2)(b)(ii)).  
That is the very thing which the covenant in this case accomplishes. 
[91] Natura also submits that the incorporation of Ladysmith’s discretion as the 
mechanism for development approval under s. 3 of the covenant conflicts with s. 122 
of the Community Charter, SBC 2003, c. 26, which provides: 
Exercise of powers by bylaw or resolution 
122 (1) A council may only exercise its authority by resolution or bylaw. 
(2) If an enactment provides that a council is required or empowered to 
exercise a power by bylaw, that power may only be exercised by bylaw. 
(3) If a council may exercise a power by resolution, that power may also 
be exercised by bylaw. 
(4) An act or proceeding of a council is not valid unless it is authorized or 
adopted by bylaw or resolution at a council meeting. 
[92] I disagree that there is any such conflict.  Section 219(2)(b) of the Land Title 
Act expressly permits a municipality to enter into covenants which govern 
circumstances in which land can be developed.  Section 122 of the Community 
Charter merely requires that a municipality, when entering into a covenant, supports 
that action through a resolution or bylaw.  Similarly, where Ladysmith exercises the 
discretion to approve or disapprove proposed development specifications under s. 3 
of the covenant, it may be that it must also do so through a resolution or bylaw.  Put 
differently, s. 122 of the Community Charter defines how a municipal council must 
exercise its authority, and says nothing about the form or manner in which authority 
can be granted to a municipality. 
[93] Based on a reading of the covenant as a whole, I find that it does not fetter 
Ladysmith in any manner which would warrant its cancellation under s. 35(2)(e).  I 
therefore decline to cancel the covenant on those grounds.

[94] Natura further relies on Metropolitan Separate School Board v. Scarborough 
(1996), 30 O.R. (3d) 186 (Ct. J. (G.D.)) to say that a municipality may not include a 
term in an agreement “which it has extracted from a landowner as part of a zoning 
process that is in excess of the statutory grant of power.” 
[95] In Metropolitan, the applicant school board challenged the right of the 
respondent municipality to enforce, in the manner of a bylaw, a site plan control 
agreement relating to property owned by the applicant (para. 1).  The agreement 
included a term requiring that the applicant remove 18 portable buildings from its 
land before a stipulated date.  The applicant did not comply with that term, instead 
applying to the court for a declaration that the agreement is unenforceable due to 
inconsistency with the Planning Act, R.S.O. 1990, c. P.13. 
[96] The court held that the jurisprudence required strict compliance with the 
legislation governing the content of site plan agreements.  The statutory authority 
permitted the inclusion of terms for removal of buildings which failed to comply with 
“maintenance and repair standards,” but not otherwise.  Specifically, nothing in the 
applicable provision of the Planning Act, s. 41, authorized a site plan agreement 
which required the removal of buildings after a specified period of time (paras. 12, 
16, 19).  As the site plan agreement at issue included such a term, the court held 
that it was unenforceable. 
[97] As noted, the covenant in this case grants Ladysmith discretion in relation to 
the approval of development on the property (s. 3).  The covenant expressly relieves 
Ladysmith from compliance with the principles of natural justice or the duty of 
fairness in exercising that discretion (s. 8).  Natura therefore says that s. 8 provides 
Ladysmith with rights which exceed its statutory grant of power. 
[98] Neither party referred me to any authority or enactment stipulating application 
of the principles of natural justice or the duty of fairness to agreements respecting 
land between municipal bodies and private persons.  Assuming for the moment that 
such authority exists, and that neither natural justice nor the duty of fairness can be 
avoided through contract (Health Care Developers Inc. v. Newfoundland (1996), 136 

Natura Developments Ltd. v. Ladysmith (Town) Page 26 
D.L.R. (4th) 609 (Nfld. C.A.) at para. 46 and Terrace Bottle Depot v. Encorp Pacific 
(Canada), 2004 BCCA 582, at para. 38, LTA ref’d [2005] S.C.C.A. No. 7 appear to 
suggest otherwise), nevertheless, s. 8 of the covenant must be read together with 
s. 9.  The latter prevents the former from impacting upon Natura’s entitlement to 
natural justice or the duty of fairness.  The covenant does not therefore purport to or 
actually grant Ladysmith rights which exceed or conflict with its statutory grant of 
authority. 
[99] In the alternative, if I am wrong and s. 8 of the covenant does contravene a 
statutory enactment, I am of the view that the appropriate remedy would be 
severance rather than finding the contract void ab initio.  In my opinion, this 
approach is consistent with the majority reasoning in Transport North American 
Express Inc. v. New Solutions Financial Corp., 2004 SCC 7, at paras. 6, 42, as well 
as s. 13 of the covenant. 
[100] The petitioner also notes that there are no drawings or plans attached to the 
covenant.  I do not accept that this warrants its cancellation; particularly because it 
defines quite clearly the property encumbered, doing so by referring to its legal 
description in the title registry (whether this includes all or only a portion of the 
property is dealt with elsewhere in these reasons).  I further find that the covenant 
has not expired, given my findings with regard to ss. 35(2)(a)-(b) and (d) of the 
Property Law Act, the reference to application “in perpetuity” in s. 2 of the covenant, 
the lack of any temporal limitation in s. 4, and the fact that the covenant binds 
successors in title under s. 10. 
[101] For the reasons expressed above, I decline to cancel the covenant under 
s. 35(2)(e). 
Conclusion 
[102] I conclude that the covenant is valid and enforceable under s. 35(2) of the 
Property Law Act.
