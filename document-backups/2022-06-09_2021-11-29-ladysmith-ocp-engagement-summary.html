EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Community engagement was undertaken between 
Spring and Fall 2021, to help inform the creation 
of a vision, goals, and growth scenarios for 
Ladysmith’s Official Community Plan.
Engagement activities included two different types 
of online surveys, virtual workshops, and in-person 
events that sought to reach a broad and diverse 
group of people. Over 400 people participated 
in one of the online surveys, and hundreds more 
contributed their input in other engagement 
activities.
The ideas, insights, and experiences shared during 
this phase of engagement were diverse and 
plentiful. While everyone contributed nuanced and 
specific input, a number of themes emerged. They 
are:
The waterfront and surrounding natural areas 
are incredibly important. 
These areas include Transfer Beach, Holland Creek, 
Heart Lake, and Stocking Lake, and they are valued 
for recreation, socialization, community wellbeing, 
ecological function, and habitat for wildlife. There is 
a sense that Ladysmith’s natural setting and access 
to nature sets it apart from many other places, and 
must be both protected and leveraged.
Ladysmith’s small town character and historic 
downtown are central to the T own’s identity.
Input from community members recognized 1st 
Avenue as a social anchor, with welcoming and 
diverse local businesses that provide a unique 
experience for residents and visitors alike.
Walking and cycling infrastructure and 
connections must be improved. 
There is a need to ensure pedestrian and cyclist 
safety, and to enhance trail and street network 
connectivity. 
Better connections and transportation 
choices between Ladysmith and neighbouring 
municipalities are needed. 
These transit and active connections are required 
to improve access to employment and post-
secondary education, enhance affordability, 
support accessibility, and reduce car dependency 
and associated greenhouse gas emissions. 
Ladysmith is caring and has a strong sense of 
community. 
Volunteerism is high and people from all walks of 
life – service club members, local business owners, 
and residents in general – help each other out and 
contribute to Ladysmith’s social fabric. 
People want to see climate action taken 
seriously. 
There is interest in diverse approaches to 
addressing climate change mitigation and 
adaptation, such as renewable energy generation, 
electric vehicle infrastructure, rainwater collection, 
and urban farming.
Housing affordability must be addressed 
immediately. 
There are significant concerns that housing is 
inaccessible to a growing number or residents.

More supports are needed for people facing 
barriers. 
Related to concerns about housing affordability, 
there is also a need for greater supports for people 
facing barriers such as poverty and mental health 
challenges, as well as the organizations that serve 
them.
Managing growth and development will help 
address many needs and community goals.
There is a recognition that new growth and 
development need to respond to a diverse set of 
imperatives including affordability, diverse and 
suitable housing options, integrated destinations 
/ land uses, transportation choices (i.e. reduction 
of car dependency), and protection against urban 
expansion into natural areas. Infill and higher 
densities offer opportunities to address these 
needs.   
Relationships between Ladysmith and 
Stz’uminus First Nation should continue to be 
strengthened. 
Participants greatly value the presence of 
Stz’uminus people and culture in T own, ranging 
from events and programs, to schools and 
neighbourhoods. Some called for the indigenization 
of Ladysmith, and to make it more welcoming to 
Stz’uminus people. 
Ladysmith has many cherished places and 
destinations. 
These range from iconic local businesses and 
community facilities, to parks and natural areas. 
There is a desire for more things to do.
Community members also expressed an interest in 
seeing more diverse local businesses such as cafes, 
restaurants, and social spaces, as well as expansion 
of recreational opportunities within town. Many 
youth expressed a need for more youth-oriented 
activities and destinations.
The arts, culture, and heritage sector is valued 
and strong. 
Galleries, facilities, historic assets, community 
events, and organizations enhance quality of life in 
Ladysmith, while creating a draw for tourists and 
visitors. Many see Ladysmith as a growing hub for 
arts and culture.
This input provided by participants will help shape 
the creation of the plan in 2022.

BACKGROUND
Ladysmith Council’s Strategic Plan provides 
direction to update Ladysmith’s OCP to set the 
vision and a road map to build the community 
it wants over the next quarter century. Project 
objectives include:

 Meet legislativ

WHY WE ENGAGE
Community engagement is integral to the OCP 
process because: people deserve to be part of 
planning processes that affect their lives; people 
are experts in their own lived experiences and 
therefore have inherent expertise to share; and 
people’s input strengthens the plan and planning 
process.
PROJECT PROCESS
The OCP update is a four-phase process that will 
culminate in an updated policy document in 2022. 
Phase 1 - Gathering Knolwedge and Creating a 
Baseline included technical analysis and review. 
We are in the process of developing a vision, 
goals and growth scenarios. There will be multiple 
opportunities to engage throughout the next 
phase. The input shared in this report comes from 
engagement undertaken in Phases 1 and 2.
Visit https://www.ladysmith.ca/city-hall/OCP for 
the latest engagement opportunities and project 
updates.

IDENTITY FACTORS
T o better understand who is represented by the 
survey responses provided, we asked participants 
to self-identify across a variety of factors. 
Among equity-seeking groups, women were well 
(over) represented compared with the Census 
(2016). The following groups were significantly 
underrepresented in the survey: single parents (4% 
compared with 15%); low income residents (5% 
compared with 11.8%); international immigrants 
(1.4% compared with 12.3%); Indigenous People 
(4% compared with 8%); Black People (1.8% 
compared with 0.3%), and other visible minority 
persons (1.4% compared with 3.3%).
Available Census data do not offer insight into the 
proportion of the community who are persons with 
disabilities and/or LBGTQ2+. However 13 survey 
participants (4.6% of the total who responded to 
this question) identified themselves as persons with 
disabilities, and 21 survey participants (7.4% of the 
total who responded to this question) identified 
themselves as LGBTQ2+. 
It will be important to elevate the input received 
by these groups in the survey and to reach out to 
these voices in future engagement activities.
Please note that since not all survey participants 
provided demographic information, the proportion 
of equity- seeking groups in the survey may 
be lower than what is seen in the community/
Census.

AGE OF PARTICIPANTS
Participants were also asked to identify their 
age. Of the 261 respondents who provided this 
information, there was a slight overrepresentation 
of ages from 35-64.  
Several age groups aligned with the expected 
rates from Census data (2016), including 65-74 
and 25-34 year olds.
Children under the age of 12, 18-24 year olds 
and those over the age of 75 were the most 
underrepresented. Future engagement efforts will 
require targeted outreach to these groups. 
Specific outreach to high school students  to 
complete the survey resulted in higher response 
rates from 12-17 year olds, re-affirming that 
targeted engagement can improve diverse 
representation.

Downtown Ladysmith is the heart of the town, 
providing a pedestrian-oriented venue for people 
to gather, bump into each other, enjoy a boutique

of the downtown’s streetscape, including how 
some of Ladysmith’s streets accommodate multiple 
modes of transportation while others are more 
auto-centric in their design. The tour also looked at 
the downtown’s residential and commercial form 
and the type of land use mix that is needed in the 
future to create an environment that supports 
businesses and offers housing and transportation 
choices to residents.
T our #2: North Ladysmith / Holland Creek
North Ladysmith is characterized as predominantly 
residential with most residents living within a 
5-minute walk to parks, nature, and green space. 
Yet, due to the area’s geography, most amenities 
and services are farther away and harder to access 
by foot or bicycle resulting in more trips by car. 
This tour explored the housing and transportation 
characteristics of North Ladysmith with a focus 
on the development patterns near Kinsmen Park. 
Sixth Avenue will also be explored as an important 
corridor that provides access to schools and 
recreational destinations. The tour concluded at 
the trail head for Holland Creek at 6th Avenue and 
Methuen Street.
T our #3: Waterfront Area
The T own’s waterfront plays an important role in 
the quality of life of the community today and is 
central to the identity and success of Ladysmith in 
the future. The 2018 award-winning Waterfront 
Area Plan provides a bold vision to develop the 
waterfront area in a way that creates opportunity 
for community prosperity, ecological regeneration, 
and brownfield restoration while honouring 
past, present, and future generations. The future 
development of the waterfront area is a critical 
part of the T own’s future but there remain several 
unaddressed challenges including overall access 
to the area and the impacts of climate change. This 
tour explored the larger waterfront area including 
Transfer Beach Park, the harbour, and the industrial 
lands.
