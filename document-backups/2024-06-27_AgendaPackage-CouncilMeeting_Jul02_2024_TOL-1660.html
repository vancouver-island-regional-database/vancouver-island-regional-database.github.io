GROWTH OF A NEIGHBOURHOOD Development of the Extension coal mines and loading wharves at Oyster Harbour attracted immigrants and establishment of the Town in the late 1890s. Most came from England, Ireland, Scotland and Wales, with lesser numbers from Belgium, China, Croatia, Denmark, France, Finland, Germany, Holland, Hungary, Italy, Norway, Russia, Sweden and the U.S. This area on the edge of Ladysmith, separated from the Town’s gridiron layout by the colliery flume which brought water from Holland Creek to the coal washery at the harbour, was more rural and featured larger lots with orchards and small farms. Circa 1915, David and Julia Johnson opened a general store and Imperial Oil service station on Victoria Road (now Dogwood Drive) to serve the increasing motor traffic. Land survey of this area circa 1905 In 1931 the Johnson’s service station and store was moved to Bayview Avenue as that became the main thoroughfare into town. The store was the community hub till it closed in the early 1960s. A close-knit community developed. An area near the Johnson store on Bayview, dominated by families of Scottish descent, became known as ‘Mush Alley’ named after porridge - a staple part of the Scots diet. Augustus DeClark farmed a small holding with cows at the south east corner of Methuen and 4th Ave. In 1932 he purchased and cleared an additional 10 acres to establish a dairy. Later the land was sold for development, a portion being donated to the town by Thomas and Wilma Wickham as a park. 1932 1 = DeClark Dairy. 2 = Mush Alley. = Johnson’s Store Family picnic beside Victoria Crescent circa 1910 Outside Johnson store/service station on Victoria Road pre-1931 Jail house at corner of Bayview and Victoria Crescent circa 1902 Johnson store/service station on Bayview Road 1950s Toboganing at Mush Alley circa 1958 Bayview Road birthday party early 1950s.
