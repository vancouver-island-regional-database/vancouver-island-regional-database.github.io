RE: 2026 Q1 (Jan-Mar) Financial Update 
 
 
RECOMMENDATION: 
That the Committee receive the staff report dated May 12, 2026, regarding the 2026 first 
quarter (January to March) financial update from the Director of Financial Services. 
 
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY: 
This is the first quarterly update since the 2026-2030 Financial Plan was adopted. At the 
time of this report, collective agreement negotiations with CUPE 401, as well as initial 
collective agreement negotiations with CUPE 606 ( Firefighters), had not been 
concluded, resulting in potentially significant unknown costs. 
 
PREVIOUS DIRECTION: 
N/A  
 
INTRODUCTION/BACKGROUND: 
This is the first quarterly financial report to the Committee for 2026.  
 
 
This report reflects financial activity as of March 31, at which time property taxes had 
not yet been levied. The 2026 property tax bylaws were adopted on May 5, and property 
tax notices are in the process of being mailed. The due date for payment of all property 
taxes is July 2, 2026, after which a 2% penalty will be applied to the outstanding current 
balance. An additional 8% penalty will be applied to any current taxes remaining 
outstanding as of September 9, 2026. The interest rate charged on arrears and 
delinquent taxes is set by the Province and is currently 7.45%1. 
 
The Town is also experiencing a decrease in interest earned on investments. As of the 
end of the first quarter, interest earned total led $308,775, which is approximately 
$20,000 lower than in 2025 and nearly $149,000 lower than the first quarter of 2024. 
 
Fortis franchise fees, which represent revenue to the Town, are projected to be higher 
in 2026 than in 2025 by $14,844.

Legal fees are higher than during the same period in 2025; however, this  is partially 
offset by cost savings related to the municipal election that occurred during this period 
last year. It is anticipated that contingency funds will be required to fund legal costs, 
which are expected to exceed budget by year end. 
 
Spending for Mayor and Council is higher for the period at $60,240 (2025 – $43,059), 
reflecting that all positions are currently filled and that some conference registrations 
and accommodation costs were prepaid. Community Contributions are lower in 2026 at 
$68,970 (2 025 – $92,920), as three organizations that were removed from the 
Grant-in-Aid process and transitioned to servicing agreements are scheduled to receive 
their funding on July 1. 
 
Information Technology expenses are higher by $19,151, primarily due to timin g 
differences related to software renewals. Cybersecurity initiatives are currently 
underway. Expenditures in the Human Resources department are lower in 2026 at 
$70,921 (2025 – $86,309), as some consulting costs have been deferred until the 
conclusion of CUPE negotiations. 
 
Unexecuted annual debt servicing payments budgeted at $965,570 for the new City Hall 
project were included in the 2026 budget . These funds may be utilized in 2026; 
remaining funds will be reserved and, for the purpose of this report, do not show as 
surplus. 
 
Protective Services Projected to be $9k under budget  
 
Net Fire Department expenditures total $188,297 for the quarter (2025 – $129,804). The 
increase is primarily attributable to the addition of the Deputy Fire Chief position, the 
timing of turnout gear purchases, and the restocking of first responder supplie s on fire 
apparatus. The total number of calls increased by 13 in 2026 . The total personnel hours 
spent responding to calls and false alarms were lower than in 2025. Training hours are 
also lower this quarter, as department -wide training occurred during the same period 
last year. That training is scheduled for Q2 of 2026; therefore, no overall cost savings 
are anticipated. Overall, paid -on-call firefighter compensation is approximately $11,000 
higher for the period. 
 
Fire vehicle servicing costs, includin g debt servicing, are approximately $3,600 higher 
than the same quarter in 2025; however, expenditures remain within budget. 
 
Net policing costs are approximately $24,000 higher in 2026 compared to 2025, 
primarily due to E -Comm costs of $53,820 recorded in  the first quarter of 2026 (2025 – 
$0). At the time of this report, the Town had not yet received the RCMP quarterly

During the first quarter, additional operating expenses were incurred, with the most 
significant increases related to storm sewer maintenance and pavement maintenance. It

budget. 
 
Due to a milder winter, overtime expenditures associated with snow clearing were

$25,840 in 2026, compared to $76,330 in 2025. 
 
Subdivision fees for a development at Churchill were received in this quarter, resulting 
in the subdivision fees target  budget being met (2026 - $32,621; 2025 - $112,884). 
Vacancies within the Engineering Department during the first quarter of 2026 are 
expected to result in cost savings; however, GIS -related fees were higher in 2026 than 
in 2025.

Expenses projected to be $2k under budget 
 
The solid waste collection contractor was changed earlier this year. The new 
contractor’s monthly charges are approximately $24,000 higher than those of the 
previous contract or. While the fees charged to properties for this service have also 
increased, reserve funds remain budgeted to offset the resulting cost difference. 
 
Cemetery Operations Revenues projected to be $3k less than budget

Cemetery burials, and the associated revenues, were slightly lower than those recorded 
during the same period last year. Cemetery operational costs were higher this quarter, 
as mild weather conditions allowed maintenance activities to commence ear lier than in 
previous years.

minimal impact to the net costs. Should the gas prices remain high, t he use of 
equipment charged is anticipated to be impacted.

INTRODUCTION/BACKGROUND: 
On September 17, 2018 Council received the final report on Ladysmith's Economic 
Development Strategy. The Strategy, developed in collaboration with key community 
stakeholders, listed developing a mountain bike trail network as an “Immediate Priority 
Project” and ranked the project highest in potential economic impacts. 
 
In the fall of 2023, the Town applied for funding from the Rural Economic Diversification 
and Infrastructure Program  (REDIP) to fund a mountain bike t rail feasibility study. The 
Town did not receive funding that year but reapplied again in 2024 and received funding 
in March of 2025, though received less than the requested amount (requested $100,000; 
received $59,798).The Town’s REDIP application  was submitted with letters of support 
from Mosaic, the Coast Salish Development Corporation 1, the Nanaimo Airport, the 
Cowichan Trail Stewardship Society, 4VI (Formally Tourism Vancouver Island), the 
Ladysmith Chamber of Commerce, Tourism Cowichan, and Economic D evelopment 
Cowichan. These letters are provided for reference in Attachment B.  
 
In July of 2025 , the Town selected Econ Consulting and Island Mountain Rides to 
undertake the Trail Feasibility Study. Since then, the Consultants have undertaken 
extensive field work, analysis and consultation with local riders, culminating in the report 
provided in Attachment A. The report provides detailed maps and Class D costing for a 
viable, phased “destination” trail network. The Consultants have also taken great care to 
produce a plan that is compatible with local forestry operations (Econ is primarily a 
forestry consultancy). 
 
The report has been forwarded to the Stz’uminus First Nation and Mosaic and discussed 
with an informal group of local riders. The  riders’ group is interested in forming a non -
profit trails society and playing a major role in trail maintenance and construction once a 
trail agreement is in place.  
 
Staff have also had preliminary discussions with Mosaic about the potential for a trail 
agreement. Mosaic provided the following response:  
 
“Thank you for reaching out to inform us of Town of Ladysmith’s Mountain Bike 
Trail Feasibility Study. We understand that this study is to be presented to Mayor 
& Council on 12th May 2026. In light of this, we would like to make Town of 
Ladysmith aware that we are still working through our Re -imagining Access and 
Recreation consultation process. Our analysis is on -going with this key strategic 
document, waiting for this analysis to be concluded in the coming weeks. We would

ANALYSIS:  
The proposed trail network provides a workable design and exceptional opportunity for 
the Town, both in terms of a community amenity and an economic development 
opportunity. Networks comparable in size and geographic location have consistently 
proven to be catalysts for economic revitalization in communities that have been impacted 
by downt urns in forestry and other resource industries. Notable examples include 
Squamish and Cumberland.  
 
Although Mosaic is currently noncommittal, staff recommend completing the REDIP-
funded portion of this initiative for three reasons.  
 
Firstly, the Town’s REDIP funding has a deadline, and the Economic Impact Assessment 
is useful for making a case  for regulatory changes,  land use agreements and/or future 
funding needed for trail construction . REDIP funding was also allocated for legal work 
needed to prepare a trail agreement and is subject to the same deadline.  If the Town 
does not use all the REDIP funding, remaining funds must be returned.    
 
Second, the Town can still negotiate with Stz’uminus, the Province and Mosaic and 
prepare the groundwork for a trail agreement , ensuring that when the opportunity to 
execute a trail agreement arises, all the necessary pieces are already in place.  From 
Mosaic’s response, it appears they will be in a better position to discuss trail agreements 
and access in a few weeks, once their a ccess and recreation consultation process  is 
complete. Staff note that Mosaic’s ‘ Public Perspectives on Recreational Access to 
Mosaic’s Forests report’, published in September of 2025, lists “biking” as the second 
most common recreation al activity, second only to “walking or hiking” which, as noted 
below also often occurs on mountain bike trails.  The report also identifies “ Activity-
Specific Access Rules”—i.e. distinguishing between lower and higher impact activities—
as well as “Agreements with User Groups” and “Partnerships and Volunteers” as 
“Opportunities for Exploration”. In short, it would appear from Mosaic’s report and above 
response that the Town’s mountain bike strategy would align well with Mosaic’s interests 
in accommodating responsible recreational access.

licence W1713), Otter Point Timber Ltd (Forest Licence A74632), BC Timber Sales, as well as private land 
owned by Mosaic Forest Management and the Town of Ladysmith.  Development of a trail network will 
require agreements and cooperative planning with the Stz’uminus First Nation and each of these licence 
holders and landowners.   
The small existing trail network located west of downtown Ladysmith would serve as the best place to 
begin the formalization, improvement and expansion of a mountain bike trail network. The existing 
network is located on Mosaic lands and within the Stz’uminus First Nation’s woodlot W1713.  These 
trails, which have been created and maintained over the years by local volunteers, are primarily 
accessed from the end of Malone Rd, but also from other community access points including the 
Holland Creek Trail. 
Expansion on the north side of Holland Creek offers a
  good opportunity to grow the existing trail 
network without significant infrastructure costs.  This would be the most natural start to network 
expansion. 
Crossing to the south side of Holland Creek would all
 ow for more significant expansion of the trail 
network as well as offering an increase in the amount of multi-use trails that could connect to and 
complement the Holland Creek, Stocking Lake and Heart Lake trails. 
With this initial network planning and expansion, the Ladysmith trails will begin to attract regional 
tourists while also being a recreational resource for local residents.   
Ladysmith has the terrain, natural features, and views for mountain bike trail development.  Bordering 
the town are diverse forests, streams, waterfalls, large old trees and historic cultural features. Adding 
additional trails will allow visitors to experience these areas and features.  With continuous 
improvement and investment in trail development and infrastructure, Ladysmith has the opportunity to

This plan breaks down potential development of the trail network into phases which are numbered by

infrastructure, ease of building and contribution to an overall trail network that will support economic 
development.  
 
These findings considered many factors when proposing the trail locations.  The primary motivation was 
to provide a framework for a fun, safe and progressive mountain bike trail network.  One that captures 
some of the historical features, highlights the natural scenic beauty of the area, and provides trails with 
a range of suitable styles and difficulty ratings to appeal to visitors as well as creating a local recreation 
amenity.  
Mountain biking is, in general terms, off road cycling on bicycles (both conventional and electric assist) 
designed for rough terrain.  It has become a sport that is accessible for all ages and abilities.  There are 
several disciplines under the umbrella of mountain biking which include: 
•  Cross Country (XC): Emphasis on climbing, distance and endurance using lighter weight bikes. 
This discipline is also a  current Olympic sport. 
•  All Mountain/Trail/Enduro: Riding varied terrain on bikes balanced to provide downhill 
performance and with reasonable climbing capability.  
•  Downhill (DH): Focused on going downhill fast over steep, technical terrain.  DH bikes are 
typically heavier and not suitable for long climbs. Downhill courses are often accessible by chair 
lift or vehicle shuttles.  
•  Adaptive (aMTB): A form of cycling that uses adapted equipment such as handcycles, trikes or 
modified bikes to allow individuals with physical, intellectual or neurological disabilities to ride 
on trails. Trails suitable for adaptive cycling are usually designed to accommodate a bikes/trikes 
with a wider stance.   
The lines between these disciplines are blurred and, at a recreational level, most bikes fall into the “All 
Mountain” or “Trail” category.  Electric assist, (E-Bike) options are available for nearly all types of riding 
now and are very common on almost all trail networks.

summarizing: 
•  Potential trails and required infrastructure.  
•  Applicable land ownership or management status.  
•  Opportunities and highlights.

Authorization to build and maintain mountain bike trails on private land requires approval, usually in the 
form of a written agreement, from the respective landowners.  Recreation access agreements between 
Mosaic and other non-profit recreational groups are typically underpinned by standards for the 
management of safety and environmental risks and the assumption of liability for the proposed 
activities.  Mosaic currently has agreements in other Vancouver Island destinations to allow recreational 
access, including mountain biking in Cumberland and Errington.   
Trail construction on Crown land is authorized through S.57 of the Forest and Range Practices Act.  
Applications for trail construction are reviewed and approved by Rec Sites and Trails BC.  Similarly, these 
applications include trail construction plans, standards for construction and signage, environmental 
assessments and any necessary mitigation measures.  Approvals necessitate the creation of a Trail 
Management Plan, reporting and the procurement of Commercial General Liability insurance.  
By necessity, these requirements would need to be coordinated to ensure they meet or exceed the 
requirements of each respective land manager.  It would not be practical to have multiple trail 
management plans, construction and maintenance standards or processes.  Given the well-established 
examples of private land access agreements and Crown land trail networks, this coordination is 
considered to be within the realm of possibility with landowner consent.

Transportation Study (Watt Consulting Group) 
A traffic study focused on 6 th Avenue was completed in the first half of 2025.  The study 
included: 
 Turning movement counts at Symonds Street, Malone Road and Kitchener Street 
 Vehicle counts and speed measurement  (pneumatic tubes) between High Street 
and Gatacre Street, and between White Street and Methuen Street 
 An inventory of street parking: 173 spaces (54  parallel, 119 angled or 
perpendicular) 
 A parking utilization study 
 Analysis of ICBC collision reports in the area 
 Use of tools from BC Active Transportation Design Guide  
 
Recommendations for Design: 
1. Reclassify 6th Avenue from Local Road to Activity Street (as defined in the OCP) 
2. Implement traffic calming measures 
3. Include separated sidewalks where possible, at least 1.8m wide  
4. Consider bike lanes or a separate bikeway  
5. Give enhanced consideration to the needs of vulnerable users (young, old, mobility 
challenged)

 3.0 m wide asphalt bikeway on south side of street 
 1.5 m wide concrete sidewalk on north side of street 
 Slight increase in parking (184 spaces: 91 parallel and 93 angled)

 Replacement of storm drains in rear yards on Stillin Drive, to Tyee Creek 
 Replacement of storm drains along 6th Avenue
