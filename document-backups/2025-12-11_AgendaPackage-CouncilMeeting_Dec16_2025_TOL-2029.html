DISCUSSION: Remedial Action Requirement Substantial volumes of water have been observed traveling from the site to flow over or pool on Town roads and sidewalks and sediment from the site has previously accumulated and clogged the silt traps in the Town’s catch basins. The Town has received a complaint about the pooling around the Town’s catch basins. Staff have had Page 45 of 167

to routinely remind the developer to repair ero sion and sediment control works and monitor the site. The constant flow of water over roads and sidewalks also creates a risk of ice forming during the winter months and slips, trips and falls represent one of the most common claims against municipalities. Staff are concerned that sediment from the soil piles, combined with high runoff volumes, could clog stormwater systems and cause flooding on roads, particularly if erosion and sediment control measures are in disrepair or fail during severe weather (e.g. poly blowing off of the soil piles). Staff note that the Farrell Road stormwater system drains into a ditch along the highway which frequently overflows and causes flooding on the highway. These situations could create hazards for motorists. A remedial action requirement will remedy the above -noted nuisances and hazards because it allows the Town to complete the remedial action if the Developer fails to do so. This can be done at no cost to the taxpayer, as the money can be collected as a debt that can be added to the tax bill for the Subject Property. It is important to note at the o utset that staff are recommending a requirement to install the erosion and sediment control measures that the Developer designed and repeatedly insisted w ere urgently needed to prevent harm to the environment , damage to infrastructure and liability for the Town. Moreover, the recommended remedial action requirement allows modifications to the erosion and sediment control measures to align with the new development propos al the Developer claims to be undertaking. Subsequently, staff see no reason for the delay in completing this work (which could have been started in February of 2025) and no reason that this work cannot be completed now. Should the Developer complete the required remedial action work before the given deadline, there are no consequences for the Developer. The Town faces potential legal consequences if it knowingly allows the above -noted conditions to persist (e.g. if a person slips on ice and is injured). Although the Developer has previously asserted that the experience and efforts of their contractors and their insurance policies are sufficient to protect the Town from liability, this has not been vetted by a lawyer and staff consider these assertations to be purely speculative, providing no practical protection to the Town from liability. It is doubtful that t he Town is a named insurer in any of the Developer or contractor’s policies and these policies have not been examined for adequacy, nor ha ve the De veloper or their contractors indemnified the Town. Staff do not accept any claims that the Leave Strip, or a comparable alternative, is no longer necessary if the Developer is abandoning their original project. The Honourable David Eby The Honourable Christine Boyle, Minister of Housing and Municipal Affairs MLA George Anderson Select Standing Committee on Private Bills and Private Members' Bills December 17, 2025 RE: Bill M216, The Professional Reliance Act Thank you for the opportunity to provide feedback on Bill M216, the Professional Reliance Act. While the Town of Ladysmith recognizes the need for timely housing approvals, and the value of professional reliance, we have serious concerns that this legislation will reduce public safety, undermine oversight, increase cost and delay, and diminish professional and government accountability. Further, the Town takes issue with the way its regulations have been characterized as a “Professional Reliance” program in briefing notes and media statements prepared by MLA Anderson. Our concerns about the Bill, and alternatives that we feel offer far better value in reducing housing costs and approval times, are noted below. The Town of Ladysmith Does Not Use a Professional Reliance Model Although the Town has adopted innovative tools that support the use of PGA professionals to expedite permit processing, it has not adopted a professional reliance model, or any other model that is consistent with what is envisioned under Bill M216. The Certified Professional Program under the Town’s Building & Plumbing Bylaw is at the discretion of the Town’s building inspector. Certified Professionals must meet specified requirements (e.g. providing proof of insurance), the process may be subject to revocations, and Certified Professionals can be suspended from participating in the program under specified conditions. The Town’s Development Approval Information Bylaw allows the Town to require Development Approval Information under section 486 of the Local Government Act. It does not require acceptance of any submitted reports and specifically allows peer review of any reports submitted. Neither of the above bylaws, in our view, constitutes a professional reliance model. Professional Reliance Is Not Risk-Free While PGA professionals consistently provide exceptional advice and oversight throughout BC, no accreditation immunizes someone from mistakes, negligence or misconduct. There are both international and Canadian precedents where inadequate professional oversight of development projects has had disastrous outcomes. This includes the Elliot Lake Mall collapse in 2012 where two people were killed and approximately twenty were injured. The Inquiry into this event found systemic errors by the engineer completing inspections on the building, a lack of oversight and a dismissive attitude towards observed risks. BC’s leaky condo crisis in the late 90’s caused millions Page 70 of 167

in damage and many homeowners, saddled with massive repair costs, went bankrupt. This crisis, caused by a combination of poor design, shoddy construction and poor oversight during a building boom, resulted in many of the safeguards and oversight of registered professionals and building permitting found in the BC Building Code today. We would encourage all members of the Legislature to read both the Barrett inquiry into the leaky condo crisis and the report of the Elliot Lake Commission of Inquiry before voting on Bill M216. Conflict of Interest: the ‘Two-Masters’ Dilemma Bill M216 would effectively let developers hire professionals who certify their own work . This functions as a self-approval model. While we recognize that accredited professionals, in most cases, have codes of ethics that require upholding the public good , and take this obligation seriously, Bill M216 creates a scenario where professionals paid for by private -sector interests will, in many cases, be the sole authority in deciding how to protect the public good. This creates an inherent dilemma where professionals must decide between the best interests of the public and the interest of their clients. The option for the authority having jurisdiction (i.e. local governments) to require third-party review and oversight, which by default is prohibited under Bill M216, is the only reliable defense against these risks. Bureaucracy, Adversarial Dynamics & Illusory Efficiency Bill M216 mandates acceptance of PGA professional submissions unless “incomplete” or complained about. Peer review requires approval by a superintendent that does not yet exist. This creates more—not less—bureaucracy. Requests from local governments for professional reports are often informally made , deferring to the professional ’s inherent understanding of the work required and recognizing that changes and clarifications can be asked for later . Under the proposed legislation, l ocal governments will be forced to formally document and outline, in exacting detail, report requirements to ensure they can reject “incomplete” reports if necessary . This will take time away from local government work that drives housing starts, such as processing permits and streamlining approval processes . Moreover, a local government’s only recourse if dissatisfied with a PGA professional’s work under Bill M216 is to appeal to the “superintendent” or to file a complaint to the professional ’s board of professional governance. The former is a taxpayer-funded adjudicator that will consume resources better used elsewhere (e.g. building housing) and the latter are organizations with limited resources, with complaint processes that may be intended to govern professional practice rather than resolve what may simply be a difference of professional opinion. Though it is an imperfect system, r elying on the professional judgement of BC’s front-line regulators (i.e. local governments) to know when third-party review is necessary, or when professional reports may fall short, is a far more efficient and collaborative approach. Inadequate Indemnity & Liability Exposure The proposed legislation contains no protection against the risks of underinsured or deceased PGA professionals. This may leave local governments exposed to claims that they failed to specify suitable report parameters , or assess the completeness of reports , as a way of circumventing the exemption from liability under section 8 of the Act. Section 8 is almost certain to invite a myriad of legal challenges about whether a local government’s actions are “in respect Page 71 of 167

of a submission certified by a PGA professional”. More important than any assignment of , or protection from, liability, is the inherent moral hazard the legislation creates by assuming the type of pain and suffering noted in the above examples will somehow be remedied because a single professional may be found liable and thus may be required to pay damages if found negligent. The inclusion of liability clauses fundamentally undermines the argument that Bill M216 will not compromise safety or quality. If reliance on a single PGA professional is sufficient, why are such clauses necessary? The only true remedy from catastrophic negligence or errors is to ensure they never happen, and the most effective way to achieve this is with adequate oversight without the unreasonable restraints found in Bill M216. Interpretation of Law vs. Scope of Professional Practice Bill M216 requires local governments to accept reports of PGA professionals “as meeting permit or bylaw requirements”. These are legal instruments. Their interpretation is beyond the technical scope of work that most PGA professionals undertake. How will interpretive disputes be resolved? Will the superintendent have a judicial or quasi-judicial function? Will this include taxpayer-funded legal counsel or will the Province allow legal interpretations from an agency that lacks legal support? Will local governments or developers be forced to seek judicial review of superintendent decisions if they feel that bylaws and permit requirements have been interpreted incorrectly by the superintendent? Risks to Innovation The Act fails to recognize the benefits of third-party review and the unintended consequences of the default prohibition on requesting it. Third-party review is often used as an added assurance that novel or innovative solutions will work. It can also be a backstop to flexible, performance- based, bylaw and permit requirements. By forcing local governments to simply accept all reports or proceed through an onerous appeal process , local governments will have little choice but to draft overly prescriptive bylaws and permit requirements , and entrench themselves in conventional, or “crude but effective” solutions. This will stifle innovation, slow housing delivery and increase housing costs . As noted above, t he Town would like to keep our staff focused on issuing permits and streamlining approval processes; not needlessly micromanaging the work of PGA professionals. Alternatives In closing, we would like to reiterate that Ladysmith, like every other local government, appreciates and relies on the professionalism and expertise of BC’s accredited professionals and supports innovation in the sphere of professional reliance. To that end, we would like to offer alternatives that the Town feels offer far better value than the proposed legislation. Funding that would have otherwise been spent to establish and operate the office of the superintendent could be reallocated to these initiatives. These include: 1. Expanding the range of s tandardized report formats and methods. For example, the templates for QEP reports under the Riparian Areas Protection Regulation and Engineers and Geoscientists of BC’s Professional Practice Guidelines for Landslip Assessments Page 72 of 167

Expanding this approach formally or informally to other areas, such as traffic studies would help streamline development approvals. 2. Such a program could be modelled after the BC Building Code Appeal Board. 3. Local governments and developers often wait months for approvals from these agencies. 4. Background: The very first page of Ladysmith’s Official Community Plan states it succinctly “Ladysmith sits on unceded lands.” The second page of the OCP , explains why we make land acknowledgements citing “awareness of Indigenous presence and land rights in the territory” and how they (acknowledgments) invite us “to reflect on how we might be perpetuating colonial processes that are ongoing and from which we have benefited, as well as the changes we will make to honour the indigenous peoples and their lands which we inhabit”. By any metric - legal/constitutional, ethical, or historical - to say that Ladysmith sits on unceded Stz’uminus Territory is not just a true statement, it is a statement of the core value of Ladysmith’s overarching community Plan (see “Primacy Of This Plan” on p.131 of the OCP). Issue: Of the 16 meetings over which the Mayor has officiated: on 13 occasions (April 1, April 15, May 6, May 20, June 3, July 8, July 22, August 5, September 1, October 7, October 21, November 4, November 25, December 2) the Mayor, despite reading directly from her Agenda, omitted the word ‘Unceded’ from the Land Acknowledgement given at the beginning of these Council meetings. In the case of September 2, no record of what was actually said exists, so no conclusion as to what actually occurred is possible.
